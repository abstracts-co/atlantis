PREFACE TO THE SECOND EDITION

THE publication of a second edition of The Problem of Atlantis affords me the opportunity of thanking a very large circle of correspondents for the kind expressions of appreciation they have been good enough to send me from all parts of the world. I was, of course, well aware that the Atlantean hypothesis had a very considerable body of adherents, but I was quite unprepared for such a spontaneous and enthusiastic reception for my work as that which it has received. I may, perhaps, be justified in regarding this widespread response as still another manifestation of the existence of that “ world-memory ” of the submerged continent to which I have more than once alluded in these pages.

In the present edition I have made a number of emendations of phrase, but few of fact, the only outstanding alteration in the latter category having reference to the chronology of early Greek and Egyptian cultures. I should also like to lay stress on the omission from the former edition of the fact, brought to my notice by Mr Ralph Shirley, that Poseidon was a god of earthquake as well as a purely marine deity—a fact which gives to the Platonic account an added significance.

In The Times Literary Supplement and elsewhere correspondents suggested that the perusal of certain more or less obscure papers might enable me to modify or correct my hypothesis. But an examination of these has not altered

Nor do I feel that my position has been weakened by the criticisms of Professor Perry in The Nation, who very naturally maintains that his own hypothesis of the Egyptian penetration of Central American culture by a Pacific route explains the resemblances between Egyptian and American cultures in a more reasonable manner than my argument for a diffusion of Atlantean civilisation in the Egyptian and American areas. I do not deny that broken and fragmentary Egyptian influences may have reached America by way of the Pacific at a relatively late date, indeed I have always held that they certainly did. But I find no intermediate links in the vast distance traversed strong enough or clear enough to account for such a large measure of Egyptian influence as Professor Perry believes to have reached American soil by that route. Moreover, the cultural formula which Professor Perry associates with his '' Children of the Sun” breaks down when applied to Mexico or Central America, as I have shown elsewhere. The Asiatic penetration of America by the Pacific route was late and relatively feeble, and contained many influences other than the Egyptian, and the hypothesis which pleads for it has perforce to rely on a much more complicated argument than that which holds that American civilisation directly emanated from Atlantis.

Professor Perry alludes to what he believes to be my sudden conversion to the fact of Egyptian influence in America. But if in earlier works I had denied this, I had made the alteration in my views clear as long ago as 1916, although at no time have I given his particular theory of the penetration of Egyptian influence into America more than

Nor did any of those critics who sought to rebut my arguments traverse my statements regarding the Aurignacian or non-Egyptian origin of European civilisation and its original progress from West to East, rather than the reverse, as is.usually inferred. Many reviewers accepted my presentation of the Atlantean hypothesis without cavil as being in line with the proven facts of modern geological and anthropological research. Naturally, I am convinced that much remains to be done before the Atlantean hypothesis comes to be regarded by official archzologists more indulgently than at present, and I am accordingly only too anxious for the criticism of those whose special knowledge permits them to indicate those weaknesses which I am conscious some of my arguments must display.

PREFACE TO THE FIRST EDITION

THE purpose of this book is not so much to demonstrate the former existence of an Atlantean continent as to place the study of the whole problem on a more accurate basis than has yet been attempted in recent times. Where the weight ` of proof has seemed to me to justify such a course I have not hesitated to speak plainly and perhaps dogmatically, and - have even in places adopted the attitude of the ardent pro- | tagonist. But it is not my intention to leave the reader with the impression that I am absolutely convinced of the infallibility of every consideration I have brought forward. ' Certain of my arguments may be traversed, but I hasten to ' say that that will not in any way alter my belief in the basic truth of my contention that the wreck of Atlantis sleeps beneath the sea. A hypothesis must, in any case, stand or fall by the nature of the proof brought to its support, and this I have sought to make of a character as unimpeachable as the very difficult circumstances admit of.

_ What I have written regarding the evidence for the . Atlantean origin of the civilisations of Central America and ` Peru is the outcome of prolonged study of the problems presented by the archzology of these areas. My predecessors in this portion of the task, the Abbé Brasseur de Bourbourg, and Dr Augustus le Plongeon, were certainly precipitate, and I may have fallen into the same error. But even when farthest astray, there is considerable method in their madness. Brasseur tells us that ''a city named

What I have said regarding the movement of early civilisation from West to East may arouse opposition from the upholders of that still unofficial theory that all culture emanated from the Egypt of the Pyramid era. I do not deny that during that era Egyptian civilisation progressed slowly Westwards. But it was, as I believe, retracing its steps towards regions whence it had originally come. So far as is known, the earth's earliest civilisation appeared in. the Biscayan and Pyrenean area, centuries before it took root in Egypt, and all the arguments in the world cannot discredit that fact. Nor can they alter the equally salient fact that, the Cré-Magnon civilisation alluded to contained the germs of early Nilotic culture, and that it continued to flourish in the Canary Islands until their discovery by the Spaniards. These are, indeed, the most powerful arguments which the opponents of the Atlantean hypothesis have to confront.

I have not, like some of my predecessors, attempted to show that Atlantis must have flourished in the “ Bronze Age.” ‘The whole circumstances render such a theory untenable. The possession of metal implements is, in my view, not essential to a high standard of human culture. The ancient Maya, Mexicans, and Peruvians were without metal tools or weapons, but they were more truly “ civilised ” than many peoples with higher pretensions existing at the present time, who could not approach them in the spheres of architecture or social polity.
