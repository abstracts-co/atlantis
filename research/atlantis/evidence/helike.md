# Helike: evidence on the ground

Lane `evidence/helike`, checked 2026-11-11. Data in `helike.json`.

Helike is not proposed as Atlantis itself. The Helike Project and a BBC Horizon film (2002) call the 373 BCE earthquake and wave, in Plato's lifetime, the likely seed of the story. Plato's text never names Helike, and most classicists read Atlantis as an invention. This lane maps the fieldwork only.

## What was claimed

- Strabo (citing Heraclides, who says he lived through it): the sea rose in an earthquake and hid Helike, "two years before the battle at Leuctra", at night, with the city "twelve stadia distant from the sea" (about 2.2 km). Pausanias saw the ruins still, "effaced by the action of the sea", about 40 stades from Aigion. Diodorus: earthquake at night, then at daybreak a wave. Aelian: ten Spartan ships lost with the city (quoted second-hand).
- So searchers looked in the sea. Harold Edgerton's sonar (from 1966; the 1999 paper says 1966 to 1974) found a target under 42 m of water, about 1400 m north of the Selinous mouth, "which appeared to suggest the presence of ruins".

## What was checked

- 1973: three offshore holes at Edgerton's target gave marine silt with shells and coral. Inconclusive. A 50 m hole on the beach (S4) and three geotechnical holes (G1 to G3, 1983) showed gravel over soft clay.
- 1988: the Helike Project swept about 8 km2 of sea floor with sidescan and subbottom sonar. "No clear evidence of a city on or under the seafloor", except one structure that looks like an ancient mole at Elaion.
- 1991 to 2002: 99 boreholes on the delta (7 to 51 m deep). Pottery turned up in an area of about 2 km2 between the Selinous and Kerynites rivers, 1 to 1.7 km from today's shore.

## What was found

- Since 2000, trenches in the same ground. The best-known:
  - **Classical building corner**, trench H19, 3 m down, below the water table. Wall stones thrown seaward, worn pottery. The authors say the pattern "is consistent with" tsunami backwash. Coins date to the early 4th century and about 340 to 330 BCE.
  - **Early Bronze Age town** (about 2500 to 2200 BCE) 130 m away, 3.3 to 4.5 m down: stone houses, cobbled streets, whole pots, burning, gold and silver items.
  - **Hellenistic dye-works** (about 39 by 25 m) and a **Roman road**, traced 800 m in nine trenches and 1300 m by tomography. Both postdate 373 BCE.
  - A late Classical to early Hellenistic destruction layer (about 330 to 300 BCE) and graves, a Late Roman building at Klonis, and Geometric and Mycenaean layers at Nikolaiika.
- The authors read the boreholes as an **ancient lagoon**. The Classical and Bronze Age sites sat at its margin, and silted over. Tectonic uplift has since lifted both above sea level. A black clay layer shows a marsh over them in Byzantine times.
- The Classical layer is **buried, not drowned**: the finds are inland, not under the sea. The authors call the link to 373 BCE "merely suggestive" and the tsunami reading "hypothetical". The 2011 abstract says the Classical site "may have been destroyed by the earthquake and tsunami of 373 B.C."

## Where sources disagree

- **Distance from Aigion.** Pausanias: about 40 stades, about 7.4 km at 185 m. Soter 1999: "about 7 km". Soter 2011: "about 7.5 km southeast". Straight line from Aigio's centre (OSM) to the dye-works: 5.1 km.
- **Edgerton.** The 1999 paper says "northeast of the Selinous River mouth" (page 532) and "about 1400 m north" (page 541). Wikipedia dates his search to 1967; the paper says 1966 to 1974.
- **Aelian.** The 2011 paper cites On Animals 11.19, the 1999 paper 11.9.
- **Timing of the wave.** Pausanias: sea and quake together. Diodorus: wave at daybreak. Strabo and Pausanias also differ on whether the whole city or only the low grove went under; Soter 1999 reads Pausanias's "encircled" as part of the city staying high.
- **Wikidata** holds two points for Helike, 1.1 km apart (22.1318 E and 22.1443 E).
- **B73 horizons.** Text: ceramic horizons at 2.1, 2.9 and 4.7 m. Table I: 2.1 and 2.65 to 3.15 m. **H18 walls:** text 2.9 and 3.15 to 3.4 m; Table I "about 3.0 and 3.4 m".
- **Uplift.** Soter 1998 gives about 2.4 mm per year for the Holocene; Stewart and Vita-Finzi about 1.5 (1999 paper). The 2005 Terra Nova trench abstract gives fault slip of 0.3 mm per year, rising to 2.0 after an earthquake near 1400 BP. These measure different things.
- **Areas.** Text: black clay about 0.7 km2; my digitised shaded area is 0.87 km2. Text: sonar 8 km2; my closed outline, using today's coast, 8.8 km2.
- **Was Helike drowned?** Stiros (The Holocene 2020) argues the 373 BCE event does not fit the geology: no proven slip on the Helike Fault, no proven tsunami, part of the site reoccupied within 40 years. He proposes mudflows from a landslide dam in the Katourlas valley and floods from inland, and later argues the texts reflect a Roman-era legend. A published comment (author names not read) answers that he neglects sources and excavation data and that the Helike and Aigion faults are 5 km apart. I read only the abstracts of both sides.

## Held against Plato

373 BCE is about 13 years before the Timaeus, about 9,200 years after Plato's 9600 BCE. The Helike delta is 4.5 km wide against a plain of 3,000 by 2,000 stades; the ceramic-bearing area is about 2 km2. The authors' minimum drop of the grove ground is 3 m, drawn from Pausanias's treetops. Plato says the sea there became impassable because of a shoal of mud; the lagoon at Helike silted over, but Plato never mentions Helike.
